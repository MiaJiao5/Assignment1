# Assignment 1 — Why Are We Here?

For designers or artists, code is never just a dry technical chore; it is a brush that can turn abstract ideas into reality. Knowing how to use this brush becomes especially important. Although AI can now generate a wide range of content from human natural-language instructions, its underlying logic still rests on code. If a user has no understanding of programming and blindly lets AI do the work, it is like asking a physical education teacher to act as a battlefield general. Without any knowledge of tactics or military strategy, he will not be able to lead a team effectively or win for long.

I still remember my first encounter with computational parameterization, which reflects what Dylan Beattie (2019) said in his talk “The Art of Code”: “Code is like a snowflake, and every time you run it feels like opening a blind box.” Before executing the code, you often have no idea what the final visual result will be. This reminds me of my first semester at university, when I first used C4D to generate artistic forms through random parameters. Each time I adjusted a value, those organically arranged units combined according to a logic completely different from human thinking, often producing a strange yet compelling beauty.

As Dylan Beattie states in The Art of Code, “Code is like a snowflake, and each run feels like opening a blind box.” Before the code is executed, you often have no clear expectation of the final form it will create. This naturally reminds me of my first attempt to generate artistic shapes through parametric randomness in C4D. Each small adjustment caused those organically arranged units to combine in a way completely unlike human logic, magically colliding into a peculiar beauty. This uncontrollable randomness both sparked my curiosity and fascination with mechanical logic, and at the same time, because I knew nothing about the underlying principles at the time, those parameters felt like a dangerous Pandora’s box—I could never tell whether the result would be good or bad, and everything seemed to hover on the edge of chaos. It was precisely this sense of unfamiliarity and uncertainty that led me, for a long time, to hold a deep respect and fear for code.

In the past two years, as AI technology has developed, the barrier to programming has become much lower, and I have gradually begun to use AI to create games, videos, webpages, and other forms of content. However, in practice, the pain points remain clear. First, although AI can efficiently handle front-end visual presentation, once I need to deal with deployment, underlying hardware, or networking frameworks, I feel completely stuck. Second, when precise adjustments are required, repeated communication through natural language with AI is not only time-consuming but also inaccurate. On the contrary, if I have some understanding of programming itself, I can quickly identify and solve these problems with far greater efficiency.

Through the practice of the first two creative coding sessions, this situation improved significantly. I successfully uploaded local application files to a GitHub repository through VS Code and published a public website via Cloudflare. In the process, I gained a new understanding of local-to-cloud deployment and the execution logic of Python code, and the fear I once felt toward coding gradually disappeared through repeated keyboard work and successful deployments. This shows that only when you have a certain understanding of a programming language can you truly move beyond the limitations of tools and create with greater control, based on underlying frameworks and knowledge.

Ultimately, learning programming in the AI era is by no means an outdated practice; it is a way for creators to hold the brush that belongs to them more effectively and push aesthetics and inspiration to their fullest expression in a more efficient way. I think this is why we are here.
